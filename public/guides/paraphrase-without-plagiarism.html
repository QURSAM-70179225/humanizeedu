How to paraphrase without plagiarizing

By Qursam Bibi · 5 min read · Updated November 2026

Paraphrasing means explaining someone else's idea in your own words and sentence structure, and still giving them credit. Done right, it shows you understand the source. Done wrong, it can still count as plagiarism.

The mistake most students make: patchwriting

Patchwriting is when you keep the original sentence structure and only swap some words for synonyms. It feels like paraphrasing, but many universities treat it as plagiarism, because the writing is still basically the original author's.

Original source

Students who sleep fewer than six hours before an exam tend to perform worse on questions that require problem-solving than on questions that test simple recall.

❌ Patchwriting

Learners who rest less than six hours prior to a test usually do worse on questions needing problem-solving than on questions testing basic memory.

Every idea is in the same place, in the same order. Only the words changed.

✅ Real paraphrase

Short sleep seems to hurt thinking more than memory. According to Rahman (2022), students with under six hours of sleep before an exam could still remember facts, but struggled with problems that required working something out.

Why it works

New sentence structure, a new order of ideas, the writer's own framing ("hurt thinking more than memory"), and a citation.

The 5-step method

  1. Read until you understand it. If you can't explain the idea to a friend, you're not ready to paraphrase it.
  2. Close or hide the source. This is the key step. If the original is in front of you, you'll copy its structure without meaning to.
  3. Write it from memory, in your own words. Imagine explaining it to a classmate. Start the sentence differently from the original.
  4. Compare with the original. Did you accidentally reuse a distinctive phrase? Is the meaning still accurate? Fix either problem.
  5. Cite it. A paraphrase still needs an in-text citation, e.g. (Rahman, 2022). Changing the words doesn't make the idea yours.

Quick tricks that help

  • Change the starting point. If the source starts with the cause, start with the effect.
  • Change the sentence count. Split one long source sentence into two, or combine two into one.
  • Connect it to your argument. Add why the idea matters for your essay. That part is fully yours.
  • Keep technical terms. You don't need synonyms for "photosynthesis" or "TCP/IP". Specialist terms are fine to reuse.

When to quote instead

Use a direct quote (with quotation marks and a page number) when:

  • the exact wording is important, e.g. a definition, a law, or a famous statement
  • you're analyzing the author's choice of words
  • the phrasing is so distinctive that any paraphrase would lose the meaning

Keep quotes short and few. An essay that's mostly quotes shows less of your own thinking. See the APA citation guide for how to format them.

Paraphrasing tools: use with care

Automatic paraphrasers often produce exactly the patchwriting described above: same structure, swapped words. They can also change the meaning of technical content. If you use one, treat its output as a rough draft. Check the meaning against the source, put it in your own voice, and always cite the original.

Checklist before you submit

  • Every borrowed idea has an in-text citation.
  • Every direct quote has quotation marks and a page number.
  • No sentence follows the source's structure word-for-word with synonyms swapped in.
  • Every in-text citation has a matching entry in your reference list.
